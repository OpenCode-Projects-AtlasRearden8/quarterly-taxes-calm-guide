When to Call a Professional

Education only. This chapter is about deciding when professional help is worth it, choosing one, and what to bring. It is not a substitute for the professional themselves, and nothing here is advice about your specific situation. When this chapter says "talk to one," it means it — generally for an hour, at a scheduled time, before the problem has a tail.

The mindset first: hiring one is a purchase, not a surrender

Somewhere along the line, a lot of self-employed people absorb the idea that needing a tax professional means you failed at being in business. Reverse that: a professional is the tool you buy when the value of sleep and of getting it right becomes greater than the fee — like bookkeeping software, an employee, or a lawyer for the LLC's operating agreement.

You will use one for different hours of your year:

  • most years for the annual filing (or not — plenty of self-employed folks file on their own, and that is legitimate),
  • occasionally for a planning consult — an hour, in the fall, with your real numbers and your real questions,
  • rarely, and gladly, for the scary stuff — letters, audits, cleanups.

Green light zone (a DIY life can still work)

  • Simple business, same state, ordinary income and expenses.
  • The rhythm works, and the ledger says the numbers are boring.
  • Past the first-year cliff, and you have a filing or two behind you.
  • You read official sources for updates and it takes you five minutes of looking, not five weeks of dread.

Even green-light people still verify current rules and keep this guide's educational framing: education ages fastest where the numbers are.

Yellow light zone (worth a consult)

Consider scheduling one when any of these are true:

  • First year self-employed — the calibration is least known and the missteps are cheapest right now.
  • income is climbing fast (past your comfort, past your previous year's bracket shape by a lot),
  • you moved, or the business is now paid by clients in new states, and you have heard the phrase "nexus" whispered in dread,
  • you bought or are buying significant equipment — depreciation gets its fingerprints on several years of returns at once,
  • you are thinking about hiring someone, taking on a partner, changing the LLC's tax election, or putting yourself on payroll (see the llc-first-year-handbook for the setup side),
  • your ledger and a payer's records disagree in a way you cannot clean up in one sitting,
  • or you simply cannot figure out which forms you owe numbers on.

One hour of a pro's time in the planning season answers these cleanly. That is the professional version of "go to the doctor sooner rather than later."

Red light zone (make that call; making it is a real next step)

  • Any letter arrived from the IRS or your state (see the panic-meter worksheet, level 4 — the pace matters there).
  • A CP notice, an audit notice, anything with "examination" or a case number on it.
  • Unfiled years in the rearview, or back taxes stacking quietly.
  • Business breakups: partners leaving, an entity winding down, an acquisition or sale of the business.
  • Big life changes (marriage, kids, a home, inheritance) land in the same tax year as business stuff, and you feel the overwhelm.
  • Any of it is keeping you up at night. Seriously: this is the legitimate "call early" trigger, not the dramatic last resort.

If it is red-zone and money is part of the reason you have not yet reached out — read the fee conversation below before deciding; there is more sliding scale available than people expect.

Who these people are

There are several distinct kinds of professional, and knowing the shapes helps:

  • CPA — a certified public accountant; many run planning plus filing practices for business owners, licensed by state,
  • Enrolled agent — federally licensed tax practitioners whose only job is tax: returns, planning, and representing the taxpayer before the IRS — frequently the sweet spot for self-employed paperwork peace at approachable fees,
  • Tax attorney — the lawyer-grade tool, best for legal-adjacent monsters (disputes, entity crises, litigation),
  • Bookkeeper — not usually a tax pro, but the person keeping the ledger a tax pro can actually read (often the earlier purchase, not the later one).

The IRS maintains a public directory of credentialed preparers; verify a practitioner's credentials and standing before signing anything.

Red flags in a preparer (run-don't-walk territory)

  • Their fee is a percentage of your refund (ask instead about the flat fee).
  • They promise refunds or deductions "bigger than anyone else gets you."
  • They are uninterested in your records and happy to work from vibes.
  • They ask you to sign a blank or already-filled return to "check."
  • They refuse to sign the return or are not even asked for the identifying information that paid preparers must include. Verified preparers sign.

Questions worth asking on the first call

  • Have you worked with self-employed folks like me (or small single-member LLCs)?,
  • Do you charge flat fee or hourly, and roughly what did people like me pay last year?,
  • What do you need from me, and by when?,
  • What happens if a letter or a CP notice arrives after filing — is that included or does it bill separately?,
  • Can we do a planning consult in the fall, not just an April filing?

And the panic-plus-sanity one: "Is there anything about my situation that would make you say I need a different kind of professional?" That question has saved more money than any deduction.

What to bring to the consult (a checklist)

  • Last year's filed return (and any unfiled years in the pile),
  • the year's ledger summary (income and expenses, straight from the cashflow-simple-ledger habit),
  • the big 1099s that came in the winter pile,
  • the year's records of estimated payments already made,
  • asset purchases and capital equipment receipts,
  • the due-date calendar worksheet, filled in,
  • any letters or notices received (copies, not the originals),
  • your list of actual questions — typed, ordered, short.

A note on DIY-friendly official resources

Before (or instead of) the first consult, some homework that costs nothing:

  • the IRS website holds the plain-publication versions of almost everything discussed in this guide, published for taxpayers, updated yearly,
  • your state's tax agency has the same for its side of the rhythm,
  • reputable educational resources on payroll, deductions, and LLC taxation from the official publications (not from strangers offering you refunds).

That homework is also how you make the consult cheaper: an hour of reading beforehand turns hour two into the real questions instead of the basics.

Quick recap checklist

  • Green/Yellow/Red — I am honest with myself about which zone I am in.
  • I know the preparer types and the red flags.
  • My consult will happen in planning season, not April, whenever possible.
  • I have the bring-list ready before any appointment.
  • Panic level 4 or standing red-zone item: I call. It is a next-step, not a verdict.
