Receipts: What to Keep (Without Hoarding)

Education only. This chapter teaches habits and categories in plain English. Which expenses are deductible for your business, what counts as "ordinary and necessary," and how long the rules say to keep what — those are rules and thresholds you should verify at the IRS website or confirm with a professional. This guide will not tell you what to write off; it helps you build the filing cabinet so the answer is easy when you find it.

The one-sentence version

Your receipts and records are the story of your business money — money in, money out, and why — and keeping that story shows up twice: at filing season, and every time a notice or question shows up from a tax agency asking you to prove it. The story is also what your accountant (or future you) will want to see.

Perfection is not the goal. Consistent beats perfect. A shaggy-but-correct habit beats a beautiful system that only ran for two weeks in March 2023.

The three record families

1. Money in

  • Invoices you issued (or a running list of client payments),
  • payment processor and bank records (deposits),
  • 1099 forms that payers send you in the winter,
  • your ledger's income side — which is the ground truth, not the 1099s (the 1099 chapter has a section on that).

2. Money out

  • Ordinary and necessary business expenses, in plain terms: what a person running your kind of business would reasonably spend to keep it going, and had a business reason to spend. That "plain terms" reading is the spirit; the official definitions and any limits live in IRS publications and in your professional's hands.
  • The receipts prove: what it was, when, how much, and ideally why (which project or client). That tiny "why" note at capture time — ten seconds, done — is the difference between a filing cabinet and a mystery box.
  • Business bank/card statements. They do not replace receipts; they support them (a bank line says money left; the receipt says what it was).

3. The long-lived stuff (assets)

  • Big purchases (a computer, equipment, furniture) may not be simple expenses; they can be depreciated — spread across years — or handled another way entirely. You do not need to master depreciation to save the receipt: save it, note the purchase, and let a professional handle the how.
  • Anything that will still be true next filing season (leases, contracts, insurance policies, licenses).

The quick "keep / skip" triage

When in doubt, keep — storage is nearly free and the downside of extra is a folder; the downside of missing is real. But for a sane guideline:

KeepSkip
Anything business-bought (tools, software, subscriptions)Personal, not-for-business spending
Income evidence (invoices, deposits, 1099s you receive)Duplicates of the same receipt
Mileage log entries (date, where, why, miles)Zero-value things with no business connection
Professional, legal, educational-for-the-business costsEveryday personal spending (rent, groceries, life admin) unless a professional green-lights a specific deduction for your situation
Asset purchases with dates and amountsOld receipts for things fully depreciated or disposed years ago (check the retention term first)
Correspondence with tax agenciesJunk receipts left over from before

The last row's "years ago" hints at the real question: how long to keep stuff at all? The honest answer is: the official guidance is published by the IRS (and some situations run longer than others); the cautious pattern many careful self-employed people adopt in practice is measured in years, not months, and asset-related records get kept for as long as the asset is alive and then some. Verify the current retention guidance, and if you have space, err on keeping longer for anything tied to assets or to correspondence with tax agencies.

The ADHD-friendly capture system (no shoebox)

The system that works is the one that runs on your worst week. Aim for:

  1. One intake place. A physical tray/envelope by the door you drop the paper into, no sorting at capture. Sorting happens in 90 seconds a week, never on entry.
  2. One photo headline. Photograph paper receipts when you can (phone in the tray's general neighborhood is fine). Name or note the why in the memo field of whatever you use. The bank stamp alone is not a receipt.
  3. One folder per month on the drive. 2026-10 style. Everything October goes in 2026-10; the ledger references it; the quarterly ritual relies on it.
  4. A 15-minute monthly tidy — it is built into the quarterly rhythm too (between quarters it stays tiny): drop the tray into the folder, photograph stragglers, flag anything missing a why.
  5. One backup. Local folder plus a second copy somewhere safe. Two places, not five.

Note for the paperless-curious: whatever tool you use — app, spreadsheet, shoebox-adjacent pile — the categories and retention are what matter. Tools rotate; the habit is the asset.

Mileage and business-use notes

If you drive for the business, a mileage log is one of those records that is boring and worth it. Columns: date, destination, purpose, miles. (Your professional can walk you through the current standard-versus-actual rules and what stays available under each.)

If you claim part of your home or phone, the general shape is "business portion with records to back it up" — that is exactly the kind of thing to ask about in the consult, so skip the improvisation and write the numbers down as they happen.

Receipts of the non-receipt kind (income confirmations, tax payments)

Your records are not only expense receipts. Also keep:

  • Quarterly payment confirmations — every direct payment you made, with date, amount, and confirmation or voucher number (the calendar worksheet has a column for exactly this),
  • annual filing documents — the return you filed and its attachments; keep with the year's records in the same pattern,
  • W-9s you sent to clients (when they needed your details to pay you), and any 1099s that arrive — they are your copies of their reporting.

That pile has a name and it lives in the same filing system as everything else in this guide.

What you can stop stressing about

  • Small personal stuff that has nothing to do with the business.
  • Org tools mismatching. Some app's folder vs. your ledger — nobody audits your folder labels; auditors check records, and yours are fine if the numbers line up.
  • Old receipts that outlived the retention term (check first, retention guidance changes — verify before you shred anything).
  • Not knowing every rule today. The cabinet exists so that the rules are easy to apply when you know them.

Quick recap checklist

  • One intake tray, one folder per month, one backup.
  • Capture the why at the moment of capture, ten seconds.
  • Mileage logged as it happens (date, where, why, miles).
  • Payment confirmations and tax filings live with the year's records.
  • Retention: verified current guidance, then keep-season with discipline.
