# Set-Aside Percentages: Your Number, Not THE Number

> **Education only.** There is no magic percentage in here, because honest
> people do not publish those — your percentage depends on your income, your
> deductions, your credits, your state, and a dozen other things that are
> yours. What this chapter *does* give you: the thinking method, the
> worksheet, and a clear way to adjust your number over time. Verify current
> tax rates and rules at the IRS website and with a professional before
> acting.

## The bucket metaphor (it really is this physical)

When your business sells something, four hands reach into the money at once:

1. **The business's own share** — costs, restocking, software, everything the
   business needs to keep operating,
2. **your take-home share** — an actual income,
3. **the tax bucket's share** — the slice that belongs to the government sooner
   or later, and
4. (if you are lucky and steady) **a savings buffer** for quiet months.

The tax bucket is the hand that people forget, because the money *feels* like
it is all yours when it lands. It is not. A slice of it was never yours. That
slice has a due date (four of them, in fact — see the [estimated-tax
rhythm](01-estimated-tax-rhythm.md)), and it is called your **set-aside**.

The habit that changes everything: **when money arrives, move the tax slice
out of checking the same day**. Different account, different tab, whatever
works. The money you do not see does not get accidentally spent.

## Why percentages instead of dollar amounts

Dollar targets break every time your income wobbles, and self-employment income
wobbles. A percentage scales with reality automatically: a $300 month and a
$3,000 month both get their correct slice without you doing new math.

## How to think about picking a number

Here is the honest version: your set-aside percentage is a **setting you
calibrate**, not a fact you memorize. The inputs that push it around:

- **How much total income you have for the year** — bigger total usually means
  a bigger marginal bite from each additional dollar,
- **self-employment tax** — as covered in the [rhythm
  chapter](01-estimated-tax-rhythm.md), self-employed people generally cover
  both halves of Social Security and Medicare on business income, which makes
  the first meaningful chunk of the number bigger than a W-2 glance suggests,
- **your state** — some states add their own income tax on top; some do not
  have income tax at all; check yours,
- **deductions** — business expenses and other deductions shrink the taxable
  slice; a business with real costs needs a smaller percentage than one with
  nearly none,
- **credits and household situations** — family stuff moves the number in
  either direction,
- **your entity situation** — a default single-member LLC is generally taxed
  like a sole proprietor; S-corp or C-corp elections change the plumbing (the
  `llc-first-year-handbook` covers entity basics).

### Illustrative ranges people start with, clearly labeled as examples

You will see self-employed folks online waving numbers from roughly the mid
teens to the low-to-mid thirties **as a share of a business's net income**.

Some shorthand on why the range is that wide:

- **Lower total incomes** tend to need small slices, because after deductions
  and credits there is often little or nothing taxed at the high end,
- **modest incomes** often land somewhere in the middle of the range,
- **higher incomes** need bigger slices, and self-employment tax on its own
  already takes a noticeable bite out of net income before income tax even
  enters the picture,
- **high-cost, no-dependents, high-tax-state situations** push toward the top
  of the range.

**This is illustration, not instruction, and it is not a promise.** Your real
number comes from your real situation: previous-year taxes, a professional
consult, or careful reading of the current IRS worksheets. Use a range to
leave the starting line — then calibrate.

## The starter method (first year)

1. **Last year's total tax.** If you filed W-2-based taxes before with a real
   tax owed, the total tax line from last year is your calibration data: it is
   the *shape* of your household's tax appetite, before self-employment adds
   its special sauce.
2. **Pick a starting percentage from the illustrative range** that matches your
   best guess at your situation. First year, modest income, ordinary state?
   Many people start in the middle of the range and adjust. (Again: example
   thinking, not a recommendation.)
3. **Set the sweep.** Every payment in, that percentage goes to the tax bucket
   before the money gets comfortable.
4. **Adjust at the first filing.** After your first self-employed tax return,
   you have real data most people would kill for. Update your percentage with a
   professional, and the guessing game mostly ends.

If you are entirely new to all of it and the spreadsheet itch has not
activated yet: pick the middle of the illustrative range as an example
starting point, then let a professional tighten it. That is one consult doing
an hour of real work.

## Where the bucket money lives

A separate account beats a mental note every time. Some folks keep a plain
savings/slush account named something obvious ("TAXES 2027" is a popular
classic); some use two buckets (one federal, one state). Look for accounts
with no fees. Whatever the money earns while it waits is a bonus, not the
point; the point is that you can see exactly how prepared you are whenever you
want to, in one number.

**Do the sweep on the day money arrives, or at a set drumbeat** (weekly works)
**— pick one and let it run**. The day-of habit is the strongest one; the drum
beat is the backup for when the day-of habit fails.

## When the bucket is short anyway

It happens — a good quarter you did not see coming, a late client, a life.
The calm order of operations:

1. Calculate what *is* in the bucket right now,
2. pay what you can toward the quarter's requirement (something beats
   nothing — underpayment penalties scale down as you pay down),
3. note the gap in your tracker, adjust next quarter's sweep upward,
4. and if the gap is big or the pattern repeats, [loop in a
   professional](06-when-to-call-a-professional.md) before it grows a tail.

If you need the panic version of this in a shell-shocked moment, that is what
[the panic-meter worksheet](worksheets/panic-meter.md) is for. No judgment at
that end, only next steps.

## Quick recap checklist

- [ ] My set-aside percentage is a **setting**, reviewed at least yearly.
- [ ] I move the slice out the day money arrives (or on a set drumbeat).
- [ ] The bucket lives somewhere visible and separate.
- [ ] Short bucket: pay what I can, note the gap, adjust, ask pro if it repeats.
- [ ] I did not take a random number online as gospel. Including this one.
