# Glossary (Plain-English Edition)

> **Education only.** Every word below is explained the way a friend would
> explain it over coffee. Definitions here are for reading comprehension, not
> for filing anything: official definitions live in the IRS and state
> publications, and they occasionally differ from a convenient shorthand.
> When in doubt, look the word up at the source before acting on it.

## 1099

The family nickname for a set of "information" forms that a payer sends to a
payee (you) *and* generally reports to the IRS, describing money that changed
hands. [Full chapter here](04-1099s-decoded.md).

## Annual return

The annual document (federal; states have their own shapes) where you report
the year's income, expenses, and the [estimated tax](#estimated-tax) payments
you made, and learn what the difference is. Filing it is how the system says
"we're square" — or "here is an amount."

## 1099-K

The specific 1099 flavor used when money reached you through **payment cards
or third-party network platforms** — card processors, marketplaces, and apps.
Amounts on a 1099-K are totals that flowed through the platform, which is why
your [ledger](#ledger) matters so much for reconciliation: the platform's
totals are not the same shape as "what I earned from whom."

## 1099-MISC

The catch-all 1099 for income that is not whole-wages-shaped: rents, prizes,
awards, other odd payments.

## 1099-NEC

The 1099 flavor for services you provided as a **non-employee** — the form a
client sends when they paid you to work. In modern use, it is basically "the
freelancer form."

## Bookkeeping

The habit of recording business money as it happens: income, expenses, and
the little stories behind them. The `cashflow-simple-ledger` companion guide
teaches a simple version. Bookkeeping is not taxes; it is the input the
taxes are calculated from.

## Business expense

Money the business spent, ideally on something **ordinary and necessary** for
running that kind of business. Receipts and a note of the "why" are what
prove it was business rather than personal. Deductions rules are detailed
and change: your records keep the receipts straight; a professional handles
the rule nuance.

## CP notice

The naming pattern for the IRS's computer-generated letters (CP plus some
digits); they are usually routine — a mismatch here, a missing form there —
but they have deadlines, and ignoring them makes everything worse. See the
[panic-meter level 4](worksheets/panic-meter.md#level-4-a-letter-arrived).

## CPA

Certified Public Accountant — a state-licensed accounting professional; many
CPAs do tax planning and preparation for business owners. [Choosing
one](06-when-to-call-a-professional.md) is covered in the professional
chapter.

## Deduction and credit

Two different ways the tax system reduces what you pay: a **deduction**
shrinks the income that tax is computed on; a **credit** cuts the tax bill
itself, dollar-for-dollar-ish. Same direction, different magnitudes, both
rule-dependent and worth verifying rather than assuming.

## Depreciation

The accounting move of spreading a big purchase (equipment, machinery,
furniture) across several years rather than writing the whole thing out at
once. Your job: keep the receipts. The method: your professional's job.

## Direct Pay

The IRS's free online payment portal — one official way to move estimated or
final tax payments. Named here so you recognize it; the IRS site owns the
actual instructions.

## Due date

The day the system says the money (or the paperwork, or both) should have
landed. Deadlines for the [estimated-tax rhythm](01-estimated-tax-rhythm.md)
shift a little each year — the due-date calendar
[worksheet](worksheets/due-date-calendar.md) exists because of them.

## E-file

Filing electronically instead of by mail. Faster confirmations, fewer stamps.

## EFTPS

The Electronic Federal Tax Payment System — the IRS's older, scheduling-friendly
payment portal. Same role as Direct Pay, different shapes of useful.

## EIN

Employer Identification Number — the business's own ID number, issued by the
IRS. An LLC can get one even with no employees, and it means a client does
not need your personal details to pay you. Setup steps live in the
`llc-first-year-handbook`.

## Enrolled agent

An **EA**: a federally licensed tax practitioner whose whole specialty is
taxes — preparation, planning, and (most interestingly) *representing
taxpayers before the IRS* when things get formal. Frequently the best-fitting
and most approachable professional for self-employed paperwork.

## Estimated tax

The pay-as-you-go tool for income that gets no withholding: you pay tax on
your self-employment earnings in **chunks during the year** instead of all at
once at filing. [The rhythm chapter](01-estimated-tax-rhythm.md) explains the
schedule; this guide explains everything else.

## Extension

Extra time to *file* your annual return. Notably **not** extra time to *pay*:
payments are still due on the ordinary schedule, which is the trap behind
many extension stories. Verify rules each year.

## Federal and state tax

Two parallel systems: the federal one (IRS) and your state's, which in some
states includes income tax and (annoyingly) **its own estimated-payment
schedule**. Separate rules, separate checks.

## Gross and net income

In practice: **gross** is what arrived; **net** is what is left after costs.
Self-employment tax and income tax calculations usually start from net
business income (the details are rule-shaped and professional-approved). Your
ledger tracks both without either of you thinking about it.

## Invoice

How you *ask* for money: the send-to-client document listing what you did and
what it costs. Invoices are receipts of *income owed*, and they sit next to
receipts in the [records system](03-receipts-what-to-keep.md).

## Ledger

The ground-truth record of what the business did with money — income,
expenses, in one honest pile. The `cashflow-simple-ledger` companion guide
teaches a low-energy version. Despite what forms claim, the ledger wins
disagreements.

## Mileage log

The record of business driving: date, destination, purpose, miles. Boring,
valuable, and the first thing auditors historically poke at. Keep it as you
go, not reconstructed later.

## Pay-as-you-go

The US system's fundamental shape: tax is owed **as income is earned**, not
in one lump at year end. Employers handle it via [withholding](#withholding);
self-employed people handle it via [estimated tax](#estimated-tax).

## Postmark

The dated stamp the post office applies to mailed things. For mailed
payments, the *postmark* is generally what counts as the on-time proof — one
of those small comforts paper payers get. Verify current handling each year.

## PTIN

Preparer Tax Identification Number — the ID number anyone paid to prepare
returns must have (and sign with). Its absence is one of the classic
[red flags](06-when-to-call-a-professional.md).

## Receipt

Proof a transaction existed: the little paper (or photo, or email) that says
what, when, how much. The [receipts chapter](03-receipts-what-to-keep.md) is
the habits chapter; the receipt itself is the atom.

## Record retention

How long to keep records before the system stops saying you must. The general
shape: measured in *years*, with official IRS guidance on the details and
caution pushing most careful folks toward keeping longer — especially for
[records of tax payments](03-receipts-what-to-keep.md) and asset paperwork.

## Safe harbor

A rule shape that keeps earlier payments "safe" from the underpayment
penalty even when the year's final tax is not yet exactly known: the usual
shape is "your year's estimated payments came to roughly what you owed last
year." Exact thresholds live in the current IRS rules; the [rhythm
chapter](01-estimated-tax-rhythm.md) explains the *feel*.

## Self-employment tax

The self-employed version of Social Security and Medicare contributions:
since nobody else withholds them for you, you cover **both halves** (the
employee's slice and the employer's slice). It is a meaningful reason the
[set-aside percentage](02-set-aside-percentages.md) is bigger for self-used
income than a W-2 glance suggests.

## Set-aside bucket

The separate place where your tax percentage lives until its due date — the
whole ([metaphorical and usually literal](02-set-aside-percentages.md))
device that keeps quarterly payments from becoming an emergency. See also:
"why your checking account can be trusted with less."

## Tax year

The year a return covers — usually the calendar year for individuals. The
[estimated-tax rhythm](01-estimated-tax-rhythm.md) partitions it into
quarters; the return is the annual summary of the whole thing.

## Underpayment penalty

What happens when a year's pay-as-you-go misses the "enough" line, scaled by
how underpaid and how late — more like an interest charge than a surprise
storm. Details and the exact math are IRS-flavored; a professional reads the
form so you do not have to.

## W-2

Your employer's annual form showing *your wages and the withholding on
them*: the shape that self-employment mostly lacks. If you still have a day
job alongside the business, you get both, on separate pages of the year.

## W-9

The form *you* send to a client so *they* have what they need to pay you and
to handle their own reporting. Normal, short, and printable straight from
the IRS. [Notes on handling it safely](04-1099s-decoded.md#i-never-got-a-1099-the-calm-checklist)
live in the 1099 chapter.

## Withholding

Tax taken out of a payment *before* it reaches you — the default machinery
for W-2 payers, and the game self-employed folks play manually through
estimated payments instead.
